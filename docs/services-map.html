Harmony · platform design · the services

Fourteen services, and why not thirty

The final service structure of Harmony, derived from an exhaustive read of what the three reference systems actually expose: every one of OPERA Cloud's 44 published API specifications (4,000 operations), the 14 operation groups of the Mews Connector API plus its POS, Channel Manager and Booking Engine APIs, and Cloudbeds' ten API families (PMS v1 and v2, Insights, Accounting, Fiscal documents, Group and Guest profiles, Attribute tagging, OTA, Payments). Each Harmony service is a bounded context that one of those catalogues also treats as a unit. Nothing is "later": this is the whole footprint; the build order is only a sequence.

The reading rule

A capability became a Harmony service only when at least two of the three treat it as a unit with its own data and lifecycle. A capability that one vendor sells as a product but keeps on another product's data (OPERA's Loyalty on CRM profiles, Cloudbeds' Groups on reservations) stays a module inside the owning service.

The rules

What keeps fourteen from becoming thirty

  • One owner per fact. A room exists in Property; Reservations, Housekeeping and Rates keep copies fed by events (the way Identity already feeds hotel-role copies to the services). A profile exists in Guests; a reservation holds the profile id and a name snapshot.
  • No transaction crosses a service. Check-in is Reservations; the first room charge is Finance; they meet through an event. OPERA's csh/checkOuts does both in one call because it is one database; Harmony does it as a choreography (below).
  • Configuration belongs to the service that enforces it. OPERA keeps 453 code lists in one lov service and a …cfg API per domain; Harmony gives each service its own reference tables through the framework's CRUD, with chain templates copied to hotels exactly as OPERA's ChainConfig/HotelConfig pairs do. Identity keeps only the ten shared tables (countries, currencies, time zones, languages…).
  • Chain-wide versus per hotel. Guests & Loyalty, Sales accounts and Reports are tenant-level (one guest across the chain, as OPERA's central profiles, Mews portfolio tokens and Cloudbeds multi-property all do). Property, Rates, Reservations, Finance, Housekeeping, POS are per hotel behind the X-Harmony-Property wall Identity already enforces; a chain template at tenant level copies into hotels.
  • The business date is Finance's. OPERA keeps it in Back Office with End of Day under Financials; Cloudbeds and Mews close the day in accounting. Finance runs the end-of-day sequence and publishes BusinessDateRolled; nobody else owns a clock.
  • Integrations are adapters inside the owning service (a payment terminal is Payments', a key encoder is Reservations', a channel is Distribution's) — but the plumbing they share (webhooks/business events, external-system mappings, device commands, exports, interface monitoring) is one service, 13, as OPERA's int/intcfg/dvm/expcfg and Mews' Device integration + Exports are.
  • Modules, not services, for: loyalty (inside Guests), groups and blocks (inside Reservations), commissions, AR and fiscal documents (inside Finance; payments are NOT, see 7), Track It and maintenance (inside Housekeeping), leisure/spa/activities (inside Sales & Events as resource bookings, the Mews way), kiosk/online check-in (inside Guest Engagement), channel manager/booking engine/GDS (inside Distribution).

Check-out, as a choreography

Desk presses Check out → 5 asks 6 (sync query) whether every folio window is settled or auto-settleable → 6 settles and answers → 5 writes CheckedOut and publishes StayEnded → 6 closes the folios (FolioClosed), 8 marks the room dirty/vacant, 4 adds the stay to history, 13 revokes keys, 14 counts it. OPERA does this inside one csh/checkOuts call; the observable behaviour is identical.

Why its own service (decided by the owner, 29 October)

All four reference systems ship payments as a product with its own API and its own PCI scope: Oracle's OPI, Mews Payments, Cloudbeds Payments, Apaleo Pay. Keeping the card data, the provider adapters and the PCI audit boundary in one small deployable keeps every other service out of PCI scope.

The night

End of day as a choreography

OPERA runs End of Day as one procedure with sixty configuration topics; Cloudbeds and Mews close the day in accounting with no audit step. Harmony keeps the industry's audit guarantees without a night-audit service: Finance owns the sequence, every other service reacts to one event.

The road

Build order, and why

2 Property → 4 Guests → 3 Rates → 5 Reservations & Front Desk → 6 Finance → 7 Payments → 8 Housekeeping → 13 Integrations
                                                                                                        ───── a hotel can go live here ─────
→ 10 Distribution → 11 Guest Engagement → 14 Reports → 9 Sales, Events & Experiences → 12 Point of Sale
  • 2 first: every other service points at a room; nothing points back. It also proves the service template (gate, events, screens, suite) on the smallest domain.
  • 4 second: the first chain-wide service; proves tenant-level data behind the hotel wall, and bookings need a person.
  • 3 before 5: a booking needs a price and a policy; 3 needs only 2.
  • 5 then 6 then 7: the booking, the folio, then the money. 5 can run with a stub folio for a few bites; 6 closes the loop with the checkout choreography and the first end of day; 7 adds cards, terminals and links behind the PCI line (cash works from 6 alone).
  • 8 then 13: room status is operationally essential and hardware-free; keys and terminals need 13, which is the go-live line.
  • 10, 11, 14: the revenue, the guest and the manager, in that order of money.
  • 9, 12 last: the largest domains, each a product on its own in the three systems; both sit on everything before them.

Decided here

The calls that shape the count

  • Loyalty inside Guests, not a 14th service. Every loyalty fact hangs off the profile; OPERA's separate product still reads and writes CRM profiles. Its aggregates stay their own (Membership root), so it can be split out one day without touching the others.
  • Groups and blocks split by side: the room side (allocation, wash, cut-off, rooming lists) in 5; the sales side (lead, status, forecast, catering) in 9. OPERA's single Block API straddles both because it predates the split; Cloudbeds keeps groups in the PMS and events apart.
  • Checkout lives in Reservations, settlement in Finance. OPERA's cashiering owns checkout; Harmony's choreography above keeps one owner per fact.
  • The business date is Finance's and it is the only clock. Property keeps the calendar (day types, events), not the date.
  • Availability is a view in Rates, computed from Property's counts, Reservations' blocks and bookings, and its own restrictions; OPERA's separate par and inv are the same split.
  • Experiences (spa, golf, parking, tours) join Events, because both are time-slot bookings of non-room resources with their own calendar; Mews models all of them as one "service" concept.
  • Content rendering and devices sit in Integrations, not in each service, so one PDF engine, one printer queue, one key-encoder protocol serve all.
  • Room rotation, comp accounting, tour series, catering budgets are modules switched on per hotel, not services; one hotel in a hundred needs them.
Closed by the owner, 29 October

Payments is its own service (7). Oracle (OPI), Mews (Mews Payments), Cloudbeds (Cloudbeds Payments) and apaleo (Apaleo Pay) all ship it as a product with its own API and PCI scope; so does Harmony.

Reservations & Front Desk stays one service (5). The evidence: Mews has no front-desk API at all — check-in and check-out are Start reservation and Process reservation, state changes of the reservation; apaleo the same (reservation-actions/checkin); Cloudbeds keeps both in the one PMS API, with the desk actions (postRoomAssign, postRoomCheckIn, postRoomCheckOut) filed under Room rather than Reservation. Only OPERA separates rsv from fof — and it then puts check-out in csh (cashiering), which shows the split is a screen organisation over one database, not a data boundary. Three of four say one; the one that says two contradicts itself. The Stay aggregate stays clearly apart from Reservation inside 5, so a split remains possible if a central reservations office ever needs it.
